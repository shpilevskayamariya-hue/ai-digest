Nobody tells you how personal the exhaustion would feel. Not just tired — but untethered. Like you've lost access to the version of yourself that could think clearly, feel steadily, handle things. And you're doing all of this while being woken every two hours by someone who needs you completely.

Here's something worth knowing: your baby's sleep is not a reflection of your parenting. It's biology, still unfolding.

## What's actually happening with infant sleep

A 2025 review published in Frontiers in Neuroscience, analysing 35 studies on infant sleep across the first six months, paints a reassuring picture of what's normal — which turns out to be quite a lot of chaos.

Newborns sleep 16–17 hours a day total, but in short, unpredictable bursts. The brain's circadian rhythm — the internal clock that tells us day from night — only begins to emerge around 10–12 weeks. True day/night patterns don't consolidate until somewhere between 3 and 6 months. Before that, waking through the night isn't a sign that something's wrong. It's just where your baby's nervous system is.

The review also notes that setbacks — sleep regressions, disrupted nights after a run of good ones — are a normal part of how infant sleep matures, often tied to growth spurts, illness, or hitting a new developmental milestone. This isn't progress undone. It's progress happening.

## What this means for you

Research tracking families over two years found that around 60% of mothers get fewer than 6 hours of sleep at the 3-month mark. That number shifts significantly by 12 months as infant sleep consolidates. The exhaustion is real — and it is temporary, even when it doesn't feel that way.

Your baby's nighttime waking is largely driven by where they are developmentally, not by what you're doing or not doing. Understanding that can take a little of the weight off.

## Gentle things that can help

- **A loose rhythm, not a strict schedule.** Consistent cues — a warm bath, dimmed lights, a song — help babies begin to understand that night is for sleep, even before their body clock is fully set.
- **Earlier bedtimes often work better.** An overtired baby can be harder to settle. Somewhere between 6 and 8pm tends to work well once a baby is past the newborn stage.
- **Day/night distinction from early on.** Keep daytime feeds and play in brighter, noisier spaces. Night feeds: low light, minimal stimulation, back to sleep quickly.
- **Rest in whatever form you can get it.** A horizontal rest without sleep still helps your body. You don't have to actually sleep when the baby sleeps — but lying down matters.

---

The long nights do pass. Your baby's sleep will find its shape — gradually, imperfectly, on its own timeline. You're not behind, and neither are they. 💛
