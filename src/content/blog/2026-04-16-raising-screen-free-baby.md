Denmark, the UK, Spain, Malaysia, and several US states are all moving to restrict social media access for children under 14 or 16. It's a significant cultural shift — governments acknowledging what many parents already feel: that the relationship between children and screens has become something worth protecting.

But if your child is a baby or toddler, this conversation starts much earlier than teenager.

## What's happening — and why it matters now

These new policies are aimed at older children, but they're rooted in the same concern: that unstructured, early, and heavy exposure to screens shapes how the brain develops, how attention forms, and what kinds of stimulation feel satisfying. By the time a child is a teenager, those patterns are already well established.

Research on early childhood consistently finds that children under two benefit most from real-world interaction — with people, objects, and environments — rather than screen-based entertainment. The first year especially is a critical window for attention, language, and emotional regulation.

## You don't have to be extreme about it

This isn't about never using your phone while your baby is in the room, or feeling guilty about putting on a show during a difficult afternoon. It's about intention — having some sense of what your child's relationship with screens looks like, rather than letting it just happen.

Babies can't distinguish between "educational" and other content the way we might hope. What they can do is explore a face, listen to a voice, touch something new, or hear you describe what you see out the window. These experiences, small and ordinary as they seem, are doing exactly what a developing brain needs.

## What screen-light time can look like with a baby

- Talking, narrating, singing — even when it feels one-sided, your voice is doing a lot of work
- Floor time with simple objects: cups, cloths, spoons, your hands
- Going outside — the visual complexity of the natural world is genuinely rich for infant brains
- Letting your baby be bored sometimes; low stimulation isn't empty, it's where focus develops

## Small things that can help

- Notice when screens become a default rather than a choice — sometimes a small shift in habit is all it takes
- Put your own phone face-down during play stretches; babies attend to whatever you attend to
- If you use a show or video, watch together and narrate what's happening
- Give yourself grace on hard days — a show that gets you through isn't a failure

The conversation about children and screens is finally getting louder. The good news for parents of babies: you're in the best possible position to set a gentle, intentional foundation before habits form.
