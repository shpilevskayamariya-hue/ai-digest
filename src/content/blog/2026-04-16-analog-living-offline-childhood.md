Something quiet is happening among parents right now. Amid the noise of apps, alerts, and an always-on world, more and more families are choosing to slow down — to put the phone down and pick up a board game, go for an unhurried walk, or just sit on the floor with their baby and a wooden spoon.

It's being called "analog living." And it's not a fringe movement anymore.

## What analog living actually means

It doesn't mean throwing out the television or going off the grid. For most families, it's far more gentle than that — a conscious effort to let childhood unfold a little more slowly, with a little more space for boredom, imagination, and unmediated experience.

The trend has been building for a few years, partly inspired by research (including Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation*) exploring the links between heavy screen use and anxiety in children and teenagers. But parents aren't waiting for policy changes — they're making quiet shifts in their own homes.

## Why it resonates with parents of babies and toddlers

If your baby is under two, this conversation is especially relevant. The first two years of life are when habits, patterns, and sensory preferences are being laid down. Babies don't need screens — they need faces, voices, textures, and movement. That's not a criticism of anyone who's used a tablet to get through a difficult afternoon. It's just a reminder that the simplest things are often the most nourishing.

Board games are for older kids, but the principle scales down easily. Peek-a-boo. A pot and a wooden spoon. A walk where you narrate what you see. A moment sitting in the garden with no agenda. These are exactly the kinds of play that babies thrive on.

## Small things that can help

- Try one phone-free stretch per day — even just 30 minutes during floor play
- Let your baby be bored sometimes; that low-stimulation space is where curiosity grows
- When you go out, leave the phone in your bag for the first part of the walk
- Notice what your baby responds to most — often it's your voice and your face, not a screen

---

You don't have to opt out of technology to offer your child a slower start. Even small, intentional pockets of quiet make a difference.
