You grew a person. Your body reorganised itself around that task for nine months, then did something extraordinary to bring them into the world. And now — in the weeks and months after — you're living in a body that doesn't quite feel like yours yet.

Maybe it's the softness where there wasn't softness before. The way nothing fits the same. The scar, or the tenderness, or the leaking, or the hair falling out. The feeling of being simultaneously disconnected from your body and more aware of it than ever.

Nobody really prepares you for this part. And the world outside certainly doesn't help.

## What research says about postpartum bodies

A 2025 qualitative study published in the *Health Promotion Journal of Australia* gathered the experiences of new mothers talking honestly about their bodies after birth. The title tells you something: *"I've grown a human, but I don't feel like 'me' anymore."*

What researchers found was a complicated mix of pride and disconnection, function and frustration. Many mothers described shifting gradually toward what the researchers called "body neutrality" — not loving every change, but coming to see their body's purpose differently. One mother's words stood out: *"My body's purpose has changed now; other people's opinions don't hold as much weight."*

That shift doesn't happen overnight. And it doesn't happen at all when you're being told — by social media, by magazines, by well-meaning people — that recovery means returning to how you looked before.

## The problem with "bounce back" culture

The pressure to lose the "baby weight" quickly, to look recovered before you feel it, to perform wellness while healing — this has real consequences. Research consistently links postpartum body image concerns to depression and anxiety. The "bounce back" narrative doesn't motivate recovery. It gets in the way of it.

The honest timeline is longer and messier than anyone tends to say out loud. Physical recovery from birth — whether vaginal or caesarean — typically takes months, not weeks. Hormonal shifts continue for as long as you're breastfeeding. Your core and pelvic floor need real, intentional rehabilitation.

And your sense of self — who you are inside this changed body — takes even longer to settle.

## Small things that can help

- Name what you're feeling about your body without judging it — it doesn't have to be positive to be valid
- Clothes that fit your body now (not aspirationally) make an enormous practical difference
- If something physical feels wrong — pain, incontinence, prolapse symptoms — a women's health physiotherapist can help; this isn't something to just live with
- Be cautious with social media that sells recovery aesthetics — it's rarely representative of real timelines
- Give yourself the same grace you'd give a friend who had just done something enormous

---

You don't have to love your postpartum body. You don't have to be grateful for every change. You're allowed to feel complicated about it — while also recognising that this body did something remarkable. 💛
