There's a particular kind of exhaustion that goes beyond needing sleep. You go through the motions — feeding, soothing, responding — but something inside feels hollowed out. You love your baby, and yet you're running on nothing. You wonder if this is just what motherhood is, or if something is wrong with you.

Nothing is wrong with you. What you might be experiencing has a name: parental burnout. And researchers are paying close attention to it.

## What burnout actually looks like

A large systematic review published in BMC Public Health in 2024, drawing on 26 studies and over 1.2 million parents, found that burnout in parents follows a recognisable pattern. It typically begins with emotional exhaustion — a deep, persistent depletion that rest alone doesn't fix. Over time, this can shift into emotional distancing, where you feel less present or connected than you want to be. Eventually, there can be a loss of a sense of accomplishment in parenting — feeling like you're doing everything and it's never enough.

Mothers report significantly higher rates of burnout than fathers. In Western countries, around 9% of parents experience it — and that number likely undercounts the mothers who are just quietly getting through each day.

## It's not about trying harder

Burnout isn't caused by loving your child too little or managing your time badly. Research points to perfectionism, limited social support, and doing the emotional and physical work of parenting largely alone as the main drivers.

A 2025 study of first-time parents found something worth sitting with: when parents felt like a genuine team — sharing the weight, communicating about it — burnout was significantly less likely. This isn't about blame. It's a reminder that this was never meant to be done by one person.

## Small things that can help

- **Lower the internal standard, not just the to-do list.** Perfectionism feeds burnout more than a messy house does. "Good enough" is genuinely good enough.
- **Accept specific help.** Not "let me know if you need anything" — but yes to the meal, the hour off, the shoulder.
- **Self-compassion is not indulgence.** Research consistently identifies it as a protective factor against burnout. Speak to yourself the way you'd speak to a friend in your position.
- **Find one person who gets it.** A friend, a mothers' group, an online community — being understood by someone in the same season matters differently than being supported from the outside.
- **Mention it to your doctor or health visitor.** Burnout is something healthcare providers can support, not just something to manage privately.
