You check that the baby is breathing. Then you check again. You lie awake running through everything that could go wrong tomorrow, next week, next month. You feel on edge in a way that's hard to name — not sad exactly, just tight, braced, unable to rest even when you finally have the chance.

This is postpartum anxiety. And it affects close to half of new mothers.

## It's not just postpartum depression

Most people have heard of postpartum depression — but postpartum anxiety is just as common and often goes unrecognised. A 2025 study found that nearly half of postpartum women (47%) experience some level of anxiety after birth, ranging from mild to severe.

Unlike depression, postpartum anxiety tends to show up as relentless worry, physical tension, difficulty sleeping even when you're exhausted, and a sense that something bad is always about to happen. You might feel like you're managing — because you're functioning, because the baby is fine — while carrying an enormous amount of fear internally.

The feelings are real. They're also worth paying attention to.

## Why it happens

Your brain is doing something genuinely extraordinary after birth. Hormones shift rapidly, sleep becomes fragmented, and your nervous system is suddenly calibrated to detect any possible threat to your baby. Some level of heightened alertness is biological. But when that alarm stays on — when the worry becomes constant and hard to control — it's a sign your system needs support, not just more willpower.

Strong mother-baby bonding, access to good care, and feeling treated with respect by healthcare providers all appear to be protective. These aren't things you have to manufacture alone.

## What actually helps

Research published by the National Institute of Mental Health in 2024 found that a structured approach — helping mothers identify anxious thoughts and gently question them — reduced moderate-to-severe anxiety from 27% to just 9%. This kind of support doesn't require anything dramatic. It can be delivered by trained non-specialists in just a handful of sessions.

In practical terms, this is good news: anxiety responds to support. It's not something you simply have to endure.

## Small things that can help

- Name what you're feeling — saying "I'm anxious" out loud, even to yourself, can reduce its grip
- Notice whether the worry is about something real happening now, or something you're anticipating; anxious thoughts often live entirely in the future
- Tell your midwife, doctor, or health visitor how you're feeling — postpartum anxiety is something they can help with
- Rest, even imperfect rest, matters — a depleted nervous system has a much harder time regulating fear

---

If the worry feels constant, physical, or like it's getting in the way of enjoying any part of this time — that's worth bringing up with someone. You deserve support for what's happening inside you, not just for how the baby is doing. 💛
