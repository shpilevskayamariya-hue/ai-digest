Nobody told you it might hurt this much. Or that latching could be a skill that takes weeks to learn, from someone who has never done it before, while running on almost no sleep. Or that your milk might come in slowly, or abundantly and painfully, or somewhere in between — and that none of it would feel like the calm, natural thing you were led to expect.

If breastfeeding didn't go the way you hoped — or isn't going the way you hoped right now — you're not alone. And you didn't fail.

## The gap between expectation and reality

A major global review published in 2024 in partnership with WHO and UNICEF found that only around 48% of mothers manage exclusive breastfeeding — well below the targets set by global health bodies. The researchers were clear about why: inadequately trained hospital staff, a lack of maternity leave, early separation of mothers and babies after birth, and relentless formula marketing. Structural failures, not maternal ones.

The same review noted something that doesn't get said often enough: breastfeeding is not necessarily a natural instinct for many women, and it can be emotionally taxing. The fact that it's physically possible doesn't mean it's easy, or that it will work without significant support.

## What actually gets in the way

Pain in the early weeks. Low supply, or oversupply. Babies who struggle to latch, or who latch but aren't transferring milk well. Mastitis. Returning to work. Exhaustion so deep that formula starts to feel like survival, not choice.

All of these are real. All of them have led mothers to stop breastfeeding earlier than planned — and nearly half of mothers do. When the research says this is driven by lack of support rather than individual failure, it's not just a statistic. It means that what you experienced wasn't inevitable. It wasn't your body letting you down. The system let you down first.

## Wherever you are with feeding

If you're still breastfeeding and finding it hard: it genuinely does get easier for many mothers around the 6–8 week mark, once supply regulates and latching becomes familiar. A lactation consultant — if you can access one — can make an enormous difference.

If you've switched to formula, or are combination feeding: your baby is being fed. That matters more than the method. Formula-fed babies thrive. Mixed-fed babies thrive. You feeding your baby, in whatever way works for both of you, is enough.

- If you're in pain, ask for help — mastitis and latch issues are treatable
- A lactation consultant or breastfeeding helpline can troubleshoot things your midwife may not have had time to
- If you've stopped, or are considering stopping: you don't owe anyone an explanation
