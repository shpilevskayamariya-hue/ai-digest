The US has long been an outlier among high-income countries when it comes to paid family leave — or the lack of it. But that's slowly starting to change at the state level, and the pace has picked up recently.

If you're in the middle of a pregnancy or the early months of motherhood, it's worth knowing what's now available.

## What's changed

Several states have recently launched new paid family leave programs:

**Delaware, Maine, and Maryland** have all introduced new paid leave programs, joining a growing list of states that now offer income support for new parents. Each program has its own rules around eligibility and how long you can claim, so it's worth looking up your state's specific offering.

**Colorado** made history as the first state to offer paid NICU leave — an additional 12 weeks of paid leave specifically for parents whose babies are hospitalized in a neonatal intensive care unit after birth. For families already navigating one of the most difficult starts imaginable, that's a meaningful change.

**Mississippi and Tennessee** expanded parental leave for public sector workers, meaning more people employed by state government or public schools now have access to paid time off after a birth or adoption.

## Why this matters

Paid leave isn't just a financial issue — though the financial part matters enormously, especially when income suddenly drops at the exact moment expenses go up. Research consistently shows that access to paid leave supports maternal mental health, improves breastfeeding outcomes, and gives parents more time to bond with a new baby before the pressure to return to work kicks in.

## Small things worth doing

- Check your state's official labor or workforce website to see what programs are currently available where you live
- If you're in a state without a program, check whether your employer offers any paid leave beyond what's legally required
- If you're on a NICU journey, ask your hospital social worker — they often know exactly what leave and financial support options exist
